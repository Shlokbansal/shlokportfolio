Engineering problem

The team needed a benchtop way to study communication between an implantable device and its external recharger at different relative distances and orientations. Movement-sensitive use conditions made alignment and separation important experimental variables.

The engineering task was to hold both components securely while allowing controlled changes in position and tilt, so the team could examine the communication envelope.

My contribution

I built an Arduino-controlled positioning prototype using servo and stepper motors. I wrote open-loop commands for up/down and forward/back motion and integrated separate 3D-printed fixtures for the implantable device and the recharger.

Both fixtures allowed tilt adjustment, adding orientation as an experimental variable alongside separation. I also documented hardware, sensing, and fixture issues, helped verify corrective changes, authored SOPs, and contributed to IEC 60601-aligned test protocols and design-history-file verification documentation.

Technical approach

  • Separate the experimental variables

    Used relative separation and orientation to structure the communication-range studies. Separate holders let the team adjust the relationship between the implanted-device component and the recharger.

  • Command translational motion

    Used Arduino commands and servo/stepper actuation for up/down and forward/back positioning. The motor commands were open loop; the prototype is not presented as a closed-loop position-control system.

  • Add adjustable mechanical interfaces

    Integrated 3D-printed holders for both components, each with tilt adjustment. The fixtures established the physical arrangement needed to vary separation and orientation.

  • Integrate, troubleshoot, and document

    Investigated hardware, sensing, and fixture interactions, collaborated on corrective changes, and documented setup and operation so the prototype could support the team’s validation work.

This summary describes the prototype and my contribution. It does not publish communication limits, device geometry, radio protocols, or internal performance data, and it does not claim closed-loop positioning or clinical performance.

Lessons learned

The experiment depends on the mechanical arrangement as much as on the control code. Secure component holding and independent position/orientation adjustments are central to comparing communication behavior.

Open-loop commands describe intended motion; they do not by themselves establish positioning accuracy. The fixture arrangement, setup documentation, and checks on implemented changes therefore matter when interpreting a test.
